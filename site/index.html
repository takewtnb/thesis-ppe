Introduction

The rise of the Dutch Republic in the 17th century

The rise of the Dutch Republic in the 17th century marked a large increase in the relative importance of the maritime trading sector, where long and high-mortality voyages carried out by the Dutch East India Company (VOC) were necessary to both fund the independence war against the Spanish Empire and contest Spanish and Portuguese dominance in these areas, later creating the basis for the Dutch Golden Age.

The prominence of the Dutch East India Company in the economy led to both “the departure of thousands of men of marriageable age” and left-behind married women (Schmidt, 2025, p. 20). Travellers’ accounts “portrayed early modern Dutch women as economically active, entrepreneurial, independent, and dominant” relative to their foreign countries (Schmidt, 2025, p. 17).

To my knowledge, no quantitative work exists on why the economic autonomy of Dutch women seemed higher than in other countries at the time. Moreover, none of the reviewed literature has studied the effects of prolonged male absence caused by a peacetime context rather than a wartime context.

Research question

Maritime-induced male absence and women’s economic autonomy

How did prolonged and permanent male absence resulting from voyages of the Dutch East India Company affect women’s economic autonomy in the early-modern Dutch Republic?

This presents an interesting historical natural experiment to study from a gendered perspective. While the general effects on the Dutch Republic’s economic prosperity are well known, little is known about the marginalised groups at home, and along gender lines, the group of interest is Dutch women.

Through a continuous difference-in-differences design, I exploit variation in the literacy gender gap and seafarer departures across Dutch cities as measures for respectively women’s economic autonomy and maritime-induced male absence.

Conceptual framework and data sources

The literacy gender gap as a measure of economic autonomy

To analyse women’s economic autonomy in the Dutch Republic, I use the gap between female and male literacy as an observable proxy outcome, which I argue is sufficiently representative of economic autonomy. Studying the literacy gender gap signals the relative economic position of women and men, which thus forms a crude yet sufficient proxy for economic autonomy.

Data on female and male literacy rates in the Dutch Republic are provided by Akçomak, Webbink, and ter Weel (2016), who aggregated literacy rates at the city level by differentiating between hand signatures and simple marks of brides and grooms in the Amsterdam Marriage Registers as a proxy for literacy. The place of former residence was registered in the records, allowing for literacy rates to be traced back to the cities from which the bride and the groom originated.

The ability to read and write was essential for attaining higher-level positions inside the maritime sector, which correspondingly would come with higher wages. More generally yet also more specifically for women, literacy also aided women in running or taking over businesses of their parent or spouse.

VOC departure intensity

Data on literacy rates, VOC employee records and city populations

To construct a continuous exposure measure to maritime-induced male absence, I use the enriched VOC seafarers dataset, which is constructed from 774,200 muster records of seafarers in the Dutch East India Company from 1633 to 1794 (Petram et al., 2024). I filter for disambiguated people and whether the place and date of their first departure is registered. Seafarer departures are aggregated into treatment intensity per city from 1633 to 1750 and normalised in relation to a city’s initial size.

Empirical strategy

To estimate the causal effect of seafarer departures on the literacy gender gap, I use a difference-in-differences approach that exploits variation in the number of seafarer departures across the Dutch Republic. By comparing changes in the literacy gender gap before and after the pre-treatment period of 1600 over two treatment periods, a three-period design allows for identification of the average treatment effect.

In contrast to the canonical design, I use a continuous treatment variable that allows for comparisons beyond binary distinctions between treated and untreated cities. A continuous difference-in-differences analysis compares across levels of treatment intensity, allowing for an analysis of the case where every city is at least minimally exposed to the VOC.

To make it more plausible that the conditional parallel trends assumption holds, I include interactions of longitude and latitude with year fixed effects to control for differential trends across cities, while also controlling for population growth to account for the influence of maritime commercial development on literacy gender gap trends.

Gap = female literacy − male literacy. Positive coefficients are discussed as a closing of women’s disadvantage (the gap becoming less negative / more equal), following the thesis convention.

Results

When the effects of seafarer departures are able to vary over treatment periods, the estimated effect in 1750 is more pronounced compared to the baseline and statistically significant at the 5% level while controlling for longitude and latitude. A city in which a 25% equivalent of the 1600 baseline population flowed out to sea, equal to a one standard deviation increase in the treatment, experienced a 2.84 percentage point decrease in the literacy gender gap.

Robustness checks

Robustness checks and limitations

After leaving out Rotterdam, a major outlier in the treatment intensity distribution, all treatment effects become statistically insignificant with far larger standard errors, confirming that the estimated treatment effects are highly driven by Rotterdam. This does not necessarily invalidate the previous results, but due to a lack of data availability gives little generalisable conclusions about the true strength of the average treatment effect.

  • Small sample. The sample contains 32 Dutch cities, making support for the conditional parallel trends assumption hard to establish.
  • Lack of pre-treatment periods. Pre-trend tests are impossible, making it difficult to argue that the parallel trends assumption is not violated.
  • Limited time periods. With more available time periods associated with literacy rates and seafarer departures, heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimators would be required to assess whether the findings remain robust.
  • Commercial development. Population growth is controlled for to account for the influence of maritime commercial development on literacy gender gap trends, but consistent statistics for pre-treatment population growth trends do not exist.
  • External validity. The data contain only Dutch cities, so the findings do not show whether the effects of seafarer departures are robust to variation in the religious status of cities.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion and conclusion

The findings provide suggestive evidence in favour of a salient shock to the adult sex ratio, which divulged into consequences for domestic marriage markets and labour markets that encouraged women to acquire human capital.

This thesis finds that VOC seafarer departures have a significant closing effect on the literacy gender gap. This effect is almost entirely driven by increases in female literacy rather than decreases in male literacy. It is robust to controlling for post-treatment population growth trends, but far less robust to dropping Rotterdam.

This thesis also opens up normative discussions on colonialism and marginalised groups. Marginalised groups in the age of colonialism do not merely exist in colonised areas, but also exist in colonising states. The normative perception of these results depends on how disjointed the domestic effects of a colonial-linked shock should be analysed from the oppressive effects of colonialism abroad.

Historical shocks can be equivalent to policy interventions: large-sweeping policy changes beneficial for women by, for example, affecting labour and marriage markets, can improve their economic outcomes. The study of historical shocks provides a method through which historical events can offer lessons for today’s policy.

Overall, this thesis provides valuable contributions to the economic history literature, implications for normative discussions beyond the scope of what is normally considered in the economic science and indirect insights for contemporary policy design. Through the study of a historical setting, this thesis links itself to important philosophical and policy debates today.
