The setting

A republic at sea, and women left ashore

The Eighty Years’ War against Spanish rule began in 1568. Decades later, in 1602, the Dutch East India Company (VOC) was chartered. Over the seventeenth century, long and deadly voyages became intertwined with the Republic’s fiscal and geopolitical position—and with the making of the Dutch Golden Age.

Those voyages pulled marriageable men out of cities for years at a time. Travellers’ accounts from the period described Dutch women as economically active, independent, and dominant. Historians have long noted seafarers’ wives taking charge of households, and unmarried women navigating marriage markets with fewer local men available.

What remains less settled, quantitatively, is whether places more exposed to VOC recruitment look different in women’s human capital over the long run.

The question

Did VOC exposure reshape women’s literacy?

How did prolonged and permanent male absence resulting from voyages of the Dutch East India Company affect women’s economic autonomy in the early-modern Dutch Republic?

This project treats that question as a historical quasi-experimental inquiry—not a settled natural experiment. The contribution is a city-level quantitative look at a Dutch-specific channel: marriage markets, seafarers’ households, legal capacity during absence, and demand for literate work in a maritime economy—rather than a peacetime replay of wartime sex-ratio shocks alone.

The headline result is suggestive rather than definitive. To understand why, first follow how the literacy outcome, VOC exposure, and city comparisons are constructed.

What we can observe

Gendered literacy, not autonomy itself

Economic autonomy is broader than literacy. The measurable outcome here is the literacy gender gap—female minus male literacy—interpreted as a signal of gendered human-capital acquisition, with implications for autonomy rather than a direct measure of it. Higher values mean a smaller disadvantage for women.

Literacy rates come from Akçomak, Webbink, and ter Weel (2016), who use hand signatures versus marks in the Amsterdam Marriage Registers, assigning brides and grooms to cities by former residence. That source is powerful for early-modern literacy—and selective. It captures people who migrated to Amsterdam and married there, not a full census of origin-city populations. VOC exposure could itself shape who migrates and who appears in the registers.

The interpretive mechanism still matters: literacy opened higher-skilled maritime and commercial work, and helped women run or take over businesses. Male absence is one reading of why incentives shifted; it is not what the data directly count.

VOC departure intensity

A continuous exposure, not a clean treatment switch

Exposure is built from VOC muster records (Petram et al., 2024): disambiguated seafarers with a registered first departure and place, aggregated by city of origin over the available record window 1633–1750, then divided by pre-treatment city population around 1600. Call this VOC departure intensity—a flow of contracts and persons, not a stock of absent men. Male absence, remittances, and legal capacity are interpretations layered on that measure.

How we compare cities

Dose comparisons under demanding assumptions

The design is a continuous difference-in-differences: compare how the literacy gender gap evolves after 1600 across cities with different departure intensity, with city and year fixed effects. Preferred specifications interact longitude and latitude with year fixed effects to hold differential western maritime trajectories closer to fixed. Population growth enters as a further control in robustness checks.

The estimand is a dose comparison: how much more the gap closes where intensity is higher. A one standard deviation rise in intensity equals about a 25% of 1600 population flowing out as recorded departures over the window—useful for scale, not a claim of as-if-random assignment.

Identification still requires a strong exclusion condition: after these controls, high-departure cities would otherwise have followed similar gender-gap trends. Geography is a first-order confounder—coefficients change sign once longitude interactions enter—so the association is clearly model-dependent. Departure intensity may also proxy maritime commercialization, chamber-city status, and labour-market connectedness rather than male absence alone.

Methods note: TWFE equation & gap sign

Baseline form: literacy gender gapi,t = β (Di × Postt) + city FE + year FE + controls × year + εi,t. A flexible form replaces Post with year-specific interactions for 1675 and 1750 (1600 as baseline). Standard errors are clustered by city.

Gap = female literacy − male literacy. Positive coefficients are discussed as a closing of women’s disadvantage (the gap becoming less negative / more equal), following the thesis convention.

What the estimates show

Preferred specification, female-driven pattern

In the preferred flexible specification with longitude and latitude controls, the 1750 interaction is positive and statistically significant at conventional levels: a one-SD higher departure intensity corresponds to an estimated 2.84 percentage-point smaller literacy gender gap (coefficient 11.37 × SD ≈ 0.25). The same specification with population-growth controls remains similar in magnitude. Without geography controls, the pattern does not look like this—sensitivity that marks longitude as a first-order confounder, not a minor covariate.

Where the evidence is fragile

Rotterdam first—then the rest of the limits

Dropping Rotterdam, the major intensity outlier and a founding VOC chamber city, makes the treatment interactions statistically insignificant with much larger standard errors. That is not a minor robustness footnote. It cuts both ways: Rotterdam may be where the phenomenon is most real, or the sample average may be an illusion of one city’s leverage. A cautious reading treats the result as closer to a high-exposure maritime-city story than a stable average effect across all 32 cities.

  • Small sample. Thirty-two cities, clustered errors, many controls—conventional significance is fragile.
  • One pre-period. Literacy is observed in 1600, 1675, and 1750; classic pre-trend tests are unavailable.
  • Treatment timing. Intensity is cumulative through 1750 and then used for both post years, so the 1675 coefficient can load on departures that occur after 1675. Dynamic interpretation is limited.
  • Confounding channel. Departure intensity may capture commercialization and institutions as much as sex-ratio pressure.
  • Outcome selection. Amsterdam marriage-register literacy may select on migration and marriage.

How to read it

A defensible claim

Cities with greater VOC departure intensity show suggestive evidence of later female-literacy gains, concentrated in high-exposure maritime places, under demanding identification assumptions.

That is narrower than “VOC male absence caused women’s economic autonomy to rise across Dutch cities.” Male absence remains a historically grounded interpretation; the estimates are associations from a difficult setting.

The same history also opens an interpretive question about colonialism: discourse often centres the colonised abroad, while this inquiry asks what happened to a marginalised group inside a colonising society. Any positive reading of domestic literacy gains sits beside extractive harm overseas; how far those should be weighed together is a normative judgment the regressions cannot settle.

Contemporary policy analogies are optional and conditional at best. Large labour-market disruptions can reshape gendered human-capital incentives in history; this thesis does not identify a modern policy analogue.
